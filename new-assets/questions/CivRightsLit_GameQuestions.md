## CATEGORY: Section 1983 Foundations

**Q1**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the primary federal statute that allows individuals to sue state officials for violations of their constitutional rights?

**ANSWER:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983.

---

**Q2**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** According to *Monroe v. Pape* (1961), does § 1983 only reach conduct that is officially authorized by the state?

**ANSWER:** No. § 1983 reaches unconstitutional conduct by state officials even when that conduct is unauthorized, illegal, or forbidden by state law.

---

**Q3**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Does § 1983 require a plaintiff to exhaust state remedies before filing suit?

**ANSWER:** No. § 1983 creates an independent federal cause of action, and there is no exhaustion requirement.

---

**Q4**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What three Reconstruction-era constitutional amendments form the backbone of civil rights litigation?

**ANSWER:** The 13th Amendment (abolishing slavery), the 14th Amendment (due process, equal protection, privileges and immunities), and the 15th Amendment (right to vote regardless of race).

---

**Q5**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What are the two key elements a plaintiff must establish under § 1983?

**ANSWER:** (1) The defendant acted under color of state law, and (2) the defendant's conduct deprived the plaintiff of a right, privilege, or immunity secured by the Constitution or federal laws.

---

**Q6**
> CATEGORY: § 1983 Foundations | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** 18 U.S.C. § 242 criminalizes deprivation of rights under color of law. Name one early case applying it to law enforcement officers.

**ANSWER:** *Screws v. United States* (1943), which applied § 242 to law enforcement officers who beat a handcuffed arrestee to death.

---

## CATEGORY: Color of Law

**Q7**
> CATEGORY: Color of Law | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** A private physician contracts with the state to provide medical care to prisoners. Is she acting "under color of law" for § 1983 purposes?

**ANSWER:** Yes. Under *West v. Atkins*, a private party who performs a function that is traditionally the exclusive prerogative of the state acts under color of law.

---

**Q8**
> CATEGORY: Color of Law | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** An off-duty police officer gets into a bar fight while not in uniform and not on duty. He flashes his badge to intimidate the victim. Does this constitute action under color of law?

**ANSWER:** This is a close case. The key question is whether the officer invoked or misused the authority conferred by his position. Displaying a badge to invoke police authority is evidence of acting under color of law even while technically off duty.

## CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity

**Q9**
> CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What two things must a plaintiff prove to overcome a qualified immunity defense?

**ANSWER:** (1) The officer's actions violated a constitutional right, and (2) the right was clearly established at the time of the violation.

---

**Q10**
> CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** In *Tolan v. Cotton* (2014), why did the Supreme Court vacate the Fifth Circuit's grant of qualified immunity to Sergeant Cotton?

**ANSWER:** The Fifth Circuit failed to view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party (Tolan) at summary judgment. It improperly resolved disputed factual issues — including the lighting on the porch, Tolan's mother's demeanor, the meaning of Tolan's words, and Tolan's body position — in favor of the officer seeking summary judgment.

---

**Q11**
> CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** Must there be a case directly on point to establish that a right is "clearly established" for qualified immunity purposes?

**ANSWER:** No. A case directly on point is not required if the Graham reasonableness violation is obvious on its face — that is, if "there are no substantial grounds for a reasonable officer to conclude that there was a legitimate justification for acting as she did." (*Luthje v. Kyle*)

---

**Q12**
> CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Courts have discretion to address the two prongs of the qualified immunity analysis in any order. What case established this?

**ANSWER:** *Pearson v. Callahan* (2009).

---

**Q13**
> CATEGORY: Qualified Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** In *Tolan v. Cotton*, the Fifth Circuit held Cotton was entitled to qualified immunity. What was the factual basis the Fifth Circuit relied on — and what did the Supreme Court say was wrong with that reasoning?

**ANSWER:** The Fifth Circuit relied on: a dimly-lit porch, Tolan's mother refusing orders, Tolan's words as a "verbal threat," and Tolan "moving to intervene." The Supreme Court held the court improperly credited only the officer's version on each of these points, ignoring directly contradictory evidence from Tolan's side — violating the rule that disputed facts must be resolved in favor of the nonmovant at summary judgment.

## CATEGORY: Absolute Immunity (Prosecutorial)

**Q14**
> CATEGORY: Absolute Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What kind of immunity — absolute or qualified — do prosecutors receive for actions taken in their role as advocates in judicial proceedings?

**ANSWER:** Absolute immunity. Prosecutors are absolutely immune from § 1983 suits for conduct intimately associated with the judicial phase of the criminal process.

---

**Q15**
> CATEGORY: Absolute Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** In *Burns v. Reed*, the Supreme Court held that a prosecutor enjoyed absolute immunity for presenting false evidence at a probable cause hearing. But what activity did the Court hold was protected only by qualified immunity?

**ANSWER:** Giving legal advice to police during the investigative phase. Advising police is not a quasi-judicial function and therefore receives only qualified immunity.

---

**Q16**
> CATEGORY: Absolute Immunity | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** In *Buckley v. Fitzsimmons*, a prosecutor fabricated expert testimony during the investigation, before charges were filed. Did absolute immunity apply?

**ANSWER:** No. Absolute immunity does not apply when a prosecutor acts as an investigator rather than an advocate. Fabricating evidence during the pre-charging investigative phase receives only qualified immunity.

## CATEGORY: Governmental (Municipal) Liability

**Q17**
> CATEGORY: Municipal Liability | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Under *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, can a municipality be held liable under § 1983 based solely on the theory of respondeat superior?

**ANSWER:** No. A municipality cannot be held liable merely because it employs a wrongdoer. It is only liable when the constitutional violation results from an official policy or custom of the municipality.

---

**Q18**
> CATEGORY: Municipal Liability | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** What are the main ways a plaintiff can establish a municipal "policy or custom" for Monell liability?

**ANSWER:** (1) A formal written policy; (2) a widespread unofficial practice or custom; (3) a decision by an official with final policymaking authority; (4) a failure to train that amounts to deliberate indifference to constitutional rights.

---

**Q19**
> CATEGORY: Municipal Liability | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** *Connick v. Thompson* (2011) involved a DA's office that failed to train prosecutors on Brady obligations. The Court held the office was not liable. Why?

**ANSWER:** The Court held that a single Brady violation is insufficient to establish a "failure to train" amounting to deliberate indifference. A pattern of violations is typically required to show that the need for training was obvious and that the municipality's failure to act was tantamount to a conscious choice.

## CATEGORY: 4th Amendment — Excessive Force Framework

**Q20**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What standard governs all excessive force claims arising in the context of an arrest or seizure of a free citizen?

**ANSWER:** The Fourth Amendment's "objective reasonableness" standard, as established in *Graham v. Connor* (1989).

---

**Q21**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What are the three Graham factors courts use to assess whether force was objectively reasonable?

**ANSWER:** (1) The severity of the crime at issue; (2) whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of the officers or others; and (3) whether the suspect is actively resisting arrest or attempting to evade arrest by flight.

---

**Q22**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Which of the three Graham factors has been identified as "undoubtedly the most important"?

**ANSWER:** The second factor — whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to the safety of the officers or others. (*Luthje v. Kyle*, 10th Cir. 2025)

---

**Q23**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Does an officer's good or evil subjective intent matter in a Fourth Amendment excessive force analysis?

**ANSWER:** No. Subjective intent is irrelevant. An officer's good intentions cannot make an objectively unreasonable use of force constitutional, and evil intentions cannot make an objectively reasonable use of force unconstitutional. Concepts like "malice" and "sadism" have no place in the Fourth Amendment inquiry.

---

**Q24**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Before *Graham v. Connor*, what legal standard did some courts apply to excessive force claims? Why did the Supreme Court reject it?

**ANSWER:** Some courts applied a substantive due process standard asking whether force was "malicious and sadistic." The Supreme Court rejected it because the Fourth Amendment — not substantive due process — is the exclusive framework for excessive force claims arising during an arrest or seizure.

---

**Q25**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [QUOTE]

**QUESTION:** Which case featured this language about the difficulty of the reasonableness inquiry: courts must "slosh their way through" a "factbound morass" of reasonableness?

**ANSWER:** *Scott v. Harris* (2007).

---

**Q26**
> CATEGORY: 4A Excessive Force | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** Who was Dethorne Graham and what made his case the vehicle for establishing the modern excessive force standard?

**ANSWER:** Dethorne Graham was a diabetic who was roughed up by police during an investigatory stop when officers mistakenly suspected he was intoxicated. He had done nothing wrong. His case became *Graham v. Connor* (1989), in which the Supreme Court established that all excessive force claims in the seizure context are governed by the Fourth Amendment's objective reasonableness standard, rejecting the prior subjective due process approach.

## CATEGORY: Totality of Circumstances — Barnes v. Felix

**Q27**
> CATEGORY: Totality of Circumstances | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What doctrine did the Fifth Circuit apply in excessive force cases that the Supreme Court unanimously rejected in *Barnes v. Felix* (2025)?

**ANSWER:** The "moment-of-threat" doctrine, which limited courts to reviewing only the last few seconds of an encounter — the precise moment force was deployed — and excluded all prior context.

---

**Q28**
> CATEGORY: Totality of Circumstances | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is the key rule *Barnes v. Felix* established about the temporal scope of the Fourth Amendment reasonableness inquiry?

**ANSWER:** The "totality of the circumstances" inquiry has no time limit. Courts must consider all relevant facts and circumstances across the entire encounter — not just the final seconds before force was deployed. Prior events "cannot be hermetically sealed off from the context in which they arose."

---

**Q29**
> CATEGORY: Totality of Circumstances | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** What significant question did *Barnes v. Felix* leave unresolved on remand?

**ANSWER:** The Court declined to resolve how much weight officer-created dangers should receive in the reasonableness analysis — specifically, whether and to what extent an officer's own conduct that created a dangerous situation bears on the reasonableness of the force used in response to that danger.

---

**Q30**
> CATEGORY: Totality of Circumstances | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** What were the facts of *Barnes v. Felix*? Why did the Fifth Circuit's analysis fail?

**ANSWER:** Officer Felix stopped Ashtian Barnes for toll violations. Within two minutes, Barnes restarted his car; Felix jumped onto the doorsill and fired twice without a line of sight into the car, killing Barnes. The Fifth Circuit reviewed only the two-second window in which Felix clung to a moving car — excluding Felix's earlier decision to jump onto the car in the first place. The Supreme Court held that cabining review to that moment was error; the full context of the encounter had to be considered.

## CATEGORY: Deadly Force — Tennessee v. Garner

**Q31**
> CATEGORY: Deadly Force | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Under *Tennessee v. Garner*, may an officer use deadly force solely to prevent a fleeing felony suspect from escaping?

**ANSWER:** No. Deadly force may not be used merely to prevent escape. It is constitutionally unreasonable to use deadly force to prevent the escape of all felony suspects regardless of circumstances.

---

**Q32**
> CATEGORY: Deadly Force | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Under *Tennessee v. Garner*, when IS deadly force permissible to prevent a suspect's escape?

**ANSWER:** Deadly force is permissible when: (1) it is necessary to prevent the escape; AND (2) the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others; AND (3) where feasible, some warning has been given.

---

**Q33**
> CATEGORY: Deadly Force | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What were the key facts of *Tennessee v. Garner* — and why did the Court hold the shooting unconstitutional?

**ANSWER:** Officer Hymon shot 15-year-old Edward Garner in the back of the head as Garner climbed a fence fleeing a residential burglary. Hymon could see Garner's face and hands; Garner appeared unarmed. Hymon had no articulable basis to believe Garner was armed or dangerous. The Court held the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment because Garner posed no immediate threat — preventing escape alone cannot justify deadly force.

---

**Q34**
> CATEGORY: Deadly Force | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** Did *Scott v. Harris* treat *Garner* as establishing a rigid checklist for when deadly force is permissible?

**ANSWER:** No. *Scott v. Harris* clarified that *Garner* is not a "magical on/off switch that triggers rigid preconditions whenever an officer's actions constitute deadly force." *Garner* was simply one application of the Fourth Amendment's reasonableness test to a specific context. The ultimate question in all force cases — deadly or otherwise — remains objective reasonableness.

## CATEGORY: High-Speed Pursuits — Scott v. Harris

**Q35**
> CATEGORY: High-Speed Pursuits | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** In *Scott v. Harris*, what did Deputy Scott do, and did the Court hold it violated the Fourth Amendment?

**ANSWER:** Deputy Scott deliberately rammed Victor Harris's vehicle to end a high-speed chase, rendering Harris a quadriplegic. The Court held this did not violate the Fourth Amendment because terminating a high-speed chase that threatens innocent bystanders is constitutionally reasonable, even if it risks serious injury or death to the fleeing driver.

---

**Q36**
> CATEGORY: High-Speed Pursuits | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What procedural rule did *Scott v. Harris* establish about videotape evidence at summary judgment?

**ANSWER:** When a videotape in the record blatantly contradicts a party's version of events — so that no reasonable jury could believe that version — a court need not adopt that story for purposes of summary judgment. Courts must view facts in the light depicted by objective evidence, not in the light most favorable to the contradicted party.

---

**Q37**
> CATEGORY: High-Speed Pursuits | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** Justice Stevens dissented in *Scott v. Harris*. What were his main arguments?

**ANSWER:** Stevens argued that: (a) the majority improperly usurped the jury's factfinding function; (b) the degree of danger was genuinely disputed — no pedestrians were present during the nighttime chase; (c) police sirens explained why other cars cleared the road; (d) Harris never lost control of his vehicle. Stevens emphasized that whether force warranted deadly force is typically a jury question, and the reasonableness framework demands case-by-case analysis, not per se rules.

---

**Q38**
> CATEGORY: High-Speed Pursuits | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** In the balancing analysis of *Scott v. Harris*, why did the Court factor in the "relative culpability" of the parties?

**ANSWER:** The Court held it was relevant that the fleeing driver intentionally placed himself and the public in danger through reckless high-speed flight, while those endangered by a decision to cease pursuit were entirely innocent bystanders. The Constitution does not extend "an invitation to impunity-earned-by-recklessness."

## CATEGORY: Police Canine Use — Luthje v. Kyle

**Q39**
> CATEGORY: Canine Force / Luthje | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** In *Luthje v. Kyle* (10th Cir. 2025), what were the basic facts that led the court to find excessive force?

**ANSWER:** Deputies responding to a reported burglary (broken window, suspect had already fled) deployed police dog Sig unsupervised through the broken window without knocking, announcing, or investigating. The dog bit Mr. Luthje, who was asleep in his own bed. Deputies watched for approximately one minute while Sig continued to bite him, even after Luthje screamed "I live here!" He was unarmed and entirely compliant. His driver's license confirmed he was the homeowner.

---

**Q40**
> CATEGORY: Canine Force / Luthje | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** In *Luthje v. Kyle*, Factor 1 of the Graham test (severity of crime) tilted in the officers' favor because burglary is a felony. Did this save the officers from an excessive force finding?

**ANSWER:** No. Even though Factor 1 favored the officers, Factors 2 (immediate threat) and 3 (resistance/flight) both weighed heavily for Luthje — he was asleep, posed no threat, and was not resisting or fleeing. A Factor 1 win for the officer does not end the analysis; the second and third factors can and did override it.

---

**Q41**
> CATEGORY: Canine Force / Luthje | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** What is the distinction between "pre-restraint force" and "post-restraint force" in the Fourth Amendment context, as explained in *Luthje v. Kyle*?

**ANSWER:** Pre-restraint force is force used to initially seize or subdue a suspect. Post-restraint force is force used after a suspect is already subdued or in custody. Pre-restraint: deploying a canine on a non-violent, non-resisting suspect with no warning violates the Fourth Amendment. Post-restraint: continued use of force (here, allowing the dog to continue biting a restrained person) is a separate constitutional violation — it is "beyond cavil" that continued force after a person is subdued violates the Fourth Amendment.

---

**Q42**
> CATEGORY: Canine Force / Luthje | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** What two prongs must be satisfied for the exigent circumstances exception to justify warrantless entry into a home, and how did *Luthje* apply them?

**ANSWER:** (1) Officers must have an objectively reasonable basis to believe there is an immediate need to protect lives or safety; AND (2) the manner and scope of the search must be reasonable. In *Luthje*, both failed: on Prong 1, the deputies had only a report of a broken window and a fled suspect — no ongoing violence, no screams, no attempt to investigate. On Prong 2, the manner was unreasonable: no announcement, no restraint, canine ordered to bite "whoever it found" regardless of who was inside.

## CATEGORY: Cross-Cutting Themes

**Q43**
> CATEGORY: Cross-Cutting | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** Officer A has probable cause to arrest someone for shoplifting. During the arrest, she tasers the compliant, unarmed suspect three times. The suspect brings a § 1983 claim. Does the existence of probable cause to arrest automatically defeat the excessive force claim?

**ANSWER:** No. The existence of probable cause to arrest does not automatically justify all levels of force used in effectuating the arrest. An officer may lawfully arrest someone but still use unconstitutional force. The force used must independently satisfy the objective reasonableness standard under the Fourth Amendment.

---

**Q44**
> CATEGORY: Cross-Cutting | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A court is evaluating an excessive force claim and limits its review to the three seconds immediately before the officer fired, excluding evidence that the officer had earlier provoked the confrontation. After *Barnes v. Felix*, is this approach correct?

**ANSWER:** No. *Barnes v. Felix* (2025) unanimously rejected this "moment-of-threat" approach as inconsistent with the totality-of-circumstances mandate. Courts must consider the full context of the encounter, including earlier events that bear on how a reasonable officer would have perceived and responded to later ones. The question of how much weight officer-created dangers receive remains open, but the prior events cannot be excluded from the analysis.

---

**Q45**
> CATEGORY: Cross-Cutting | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** How do the five core Unit 6 cases (*Graham*, *Garner*, *Scott*, *Barnes*, *Luthje*) fit together as a doctrinal progression?

**ANSWER:** *Graham v. Connor* establishes the foundational framework: all 4A excessive force claims use objective reasonableness from a reasonable officer's perspective. *Garner* applies it to deadly force against fleeing suspects, adding the requirement of probable cause of serious harm. *Scott v. Harris* clarifies that *Garner* is not a rigid checklist and that Graham's balancing allows consideration of relative culpability and objective video evidence. *Barnes v. Felix* rejects temporal limitations on the inquiry, requiring courts to consider the full encounter context. *Luthje v. Kyle* applies the framework to non-lethal canine force, shows how Factors 2 and 3 can override Factor 1, and adds texture on pre- vs. post-restraint force and exigent circumstances.
