## CATEGORY: Easements

**Q1**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the difference between an easement and a fee simple? What is the difference between an easement and a license?

**ANSWER:** A fee simple gives full possession and ownership of land. An easement is only a non-possessory right to *use* another's land — the owner retains possession. A license is revocable at will and is not a property interest at all; an easement is a property interest that cannot be unilaterally revoked.

---

**Q2**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — A prescriptive easement requires the user to prove exclusive use of the land, just like adverse possession.

**ANSWER:** False. Exclusivity is a required element for adverse possession but is NOT required for a prescriptive easement. A prescriptive easement requires only: actual use, open and notorious, hostile (without permission), and continuous use for the statutory period.

---

**Q3**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the difference between an easement appurtenant and an easement in gross?

**ANSWER:** An easement appurtenant benefits a specific parcel of land (the dominant estate) and runs with both the dominant and servient estates when they are transferred. An easement in gross benefits a person or entity, not a parcel — there is no dominant estate. Commercial easements in gross (like utility lines) can be transferred; personal easements in gross generally cannot.

---

**Q4**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Name the five methods of creating an easement and give a one-sentence description of each.

**ANSWER:** (1) **Express** — created by a written instrument satisfying the Statute of Frauds. (2) **Estoppel** — arises from a promise of an easement plus reasonable and detrimental reliance by the promisee. (3) **Prior Use (Implied)** — requires common ownership, an apparent prior use of one part over another, and reasonable necessity at the time of severance. (4) **Necessity** — requires common ownership, a severance that landlocked a parcel, and strict necessity (not just convenience). (5) **Prescriptive** — open, notorious, hostile, and continuous use for the statutory period.

---

**Q5**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — Under the merger doctrine, if an easement terminates because the same person acquires both the dominant and servient estates, the easement automatically revives if the parcels are later re-split and sold to different owners.

**ANSWER:** False. Merger permanently extinguishes an easement. It does NOT revive if the parcels are subsequently re-split and conveyed to different owners. A new easement would have to be expressly created.

---

**Q6**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What are "Boyack's Six -ION Matters" for drafting an easement, and why do they matter?

**ANSWER:** The six drafting considerations are: **Creation** (how is the easement formed?), **Succession** (does it run with the land or is it personal?), **Definition** (what is the scope of permissible use?), **Relocation** (can either party move the easement?), **Duration** (when does it end?), and **Termination** (what events will extinguish it?). They matter because ambiguity in any of these dimensions typically leads to litigation about scope or validity.

---

**Q7**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Nonuse alone is never enough to terminate an easement by abandonment. What additional element is required, and why?

**ANSWER:** Abandonment requires nonuse *plus* an intent to permanently abandon the easement. Courts require affirmative conduct manifesting that intent (e.g., building a structure that permanently blocks the easement corridor). The reason: mere nonuse could reflect temporary circumstances, and property rights should not be lost by inaction alone.

---

**Q8**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Only *exclusive* easements in gross can be divided and transferred. What does "exclusive" mean in this context?

**ANSWER:** An exclusive easement in gross is one where the holder has the sole right to exercise the easement — the servient estate owner has given up the right to use the easement corridor for the same purpose. Non-exclusive easements in gross (where the servient owner retains the same right) cannot be divided or transferred because doing so could overburden the servient estate beyond what was originally granted.

---

**Q9**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** O owns Lots 1 and 2. For years, O uses a gravel path across Lot 2 to reach the road from Lot 1. The path is visible and well-worn. O then sells Lot 2 to A without mentioning the path. A builds a fence blocking the path. Does the buyer of Lot 1 have an easement? Analyze.

**ANSWER:** This is an **easement implied from prior use**. The elements are: (1) Common ownership — O owned both lots. ✓ (2) Apparent prior use — the gravel path was visible and well-worn, satisfying the "apparent" requirement even to a careful buyer. ✓ (3) Reasonable necessity — the path provides access to the road; access is reasonably (though not strictly) necessary. ✓ Because all three elements are satisfied, a court would likely imply an easement over Lot 2 for the benefit of Lot 1. Note: This is *not* easement by necessity, which requires strict (not merely reasonable) necessity and landlocking.

---

**Q10**
> CATEGORY: Easements | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A holds an express easement to cross B's land. A begins using a different, wider path across B's land that provides more convenient access. B sues. What is the legal issue, and who likely wins?

**ANSWER:** The issue is **scope of the easement**. An express easement is governed by the language of the grant. An easement holder who uses the easement in a way that exceeds its defined scope is committing a **misuse**. Here, A is using a different route and a wider path — both likely beyond the scope of what was expressly granted. B would likely win an injunction requiring A to return to the originally granted path. A does not acquire rights to expanded use through convenience.

## CATEGORY: Recording Acts

**Q11**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** When does a deed become legally effective — upon delivery or upon recording?

**ANSWER:** A deed becomes effective upon **delivery** — the moment the grantor intentionally transfers the deed to the grantee with the intent to pass title. Recording protects that title against subsequent claimants but does not create the transfer. A deed that is never recorded still transfers title between the original parties.

---

**Q12**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Name the three types of recording acts and state in one sentence the rule each applies.

**ANSWER:** (1) **Race** — first to record wins, regardless of whether they had notice of a prior conveyance. (2) **Notice** — the last bona fide purchaser (BFP) without notice of a prior conveyance wins, even if they haven't yet recorded. (3) **Race-Notice** — the claimant must be *both* a BFP (no notice at time of conveyance) *and* record first to prevail.

---

**Q13**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — Under a notice recording act, a buyer who pays full value but has actual knowledge of a prior unrecorded deed still qualifies as a bona fide purchaser (BFP).

**ANSWER:** False. A BFP must (1) pay value AND (2) lack notice. A buyer with actual knowledge of a prior conveyance has notice and therefore does not qualify as a BFP, regardless of the price paid.

---

**Q14**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is a "wild deed," and why does it fail to provide constructive notice in a grantor-grantee index system?

**ANSWER:** A wild deed is a deed that has been recorded, but a prior link in the chain of title is missing from the index — meaning the grantor-grantee index has no entry connecting this deed to the owner of record. Because a reasonable title searcher searching backward through the grantor index would never find the wild deed (there's no entry for the grantor), it provides no constructive notice in a grantor-grantee system. A wild deed *does* give notice in a tract index system (organized by parcel, not person).

---

**Q15**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is the Shelter Rule, and what is its key exception?

**ANSWER:** The Shelter Rule holds that a BFP can convey their paramount (protected) title to *anyone*, including a person who had notice of the prior conveyance — effectively "sheltering" that subsequent grantee under the BFP's status. The key exception: the BFP cannot convey back to the **original wrongdoer** (the person who committed the fraud or double-conveyance). That transfer would not be sheltered.

---

**Q16**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Name the three types of notice relevant to recording act analysis and give an example of each.

**ANSWER:** (1) **Actual notice** — the buyer personally knew of the prior claim (e.g., the seller told them). (2) **Record/Constructive notice** — what a reasonable title search of the public records would have revealed (e.g., a deed recorded in the chain of title). (3) **Inquiry notice** — facts visible upon reasonable physical inspection of the property that would prompt a reasonable buyer to investigate further (e.g., someone else visibly living on the land).

---

**Q17**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** List four examples of off-record risks that can affect title even when a buyer conducts a thorough records search.

**ANSWER:** Examples include: forgery of a prior deed, adverse possession (not reflected in the records), intestate succession (title passing by operation of law without a recorded deed), implied easements (arising from prior use or necessity, never expressly granted), unrecorded tenants in possession, mechanic's liens (which may relate back to the start of work before filing), marriage rights, and faulty notarization of a prior deed.

---

**Q18**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** O conveys Blackacre to A on January 1. A does not record. On February 1, O conveys Blackacre to B. B pays full price and has no knowledge of A's deed. B records on February 5. A then records on February 10. The jurisdiction has a **notice** recording act. Who holds paramount title?

**ANSWER:** **B** holds paramount title. Under a notice act, the last BFP without notice at the time of conveyance wins. When B took the deed on February 1, A had not yet recorded — so B had no record notice. B paid full value and had no actual or inquiry notice. B qualifies as a BFP. Under a notice act, B's status is fixed at the moment of conveyance; B does not need to record first. B wins.

---

**Q19**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** Same facts as above — O conveys to A (unrecorded), then to B (BFP, full price, no notice) — but now the jurisdiction has a **race-notice** act. B does not record. A records on March 1. B records on March 10. Who wins?

**ANSWER:** **A** wins. Under race-notice, a claimant must be *both* a BFP *and* record first. B was a BFP (no notice at time of conveyance, paid value) ✓. But B did not record first — A recorded on March 1, before B's March 10 recording. ✗ Because B fails the "record first" prong, B cannot invoke the recording act's protection. A, as the first-in-time grantee, wins.

---

**Q20**
> CATEGORY: Recording Acts | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** X is a BFP who holds good title to Greenacre under a notice act. X then conveys Greenacre to Y, who knew about a prior unrecorded claim by Z when they purchased from X. Can Y rely on X's BFP status to take free of Z's claim?

**ANSWER:** Yes — under the **Shelter Rule**, Y takes free of Z's claim. Y is "sheltered" by X's BFP status. The rule exists to allow BFPs to freely transfer their title; if subsequent buyers with notice could not take good title, the BFP's title would be unmarketable. The only exception — conveying back to the original wrongdoer — does not apply here.

## CATEGORY: Mortgages

**Q21**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the difference between a mortgage note and the mortgage instrument itself? Can one exist without the other?

**ANSWER:** The **note** is the borrower's personal promise to repay the debt — it creates the personal obligation. The **mortgage** is a lien on the real property securing that debt. A note without a mortgage can exist (an unsecured loan). A mortgage without a note cannot exist — there must be an underlying debt obligation for the lien to secure. The two travel together in most transactions.

---

**Q22**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — In a lien theory state, the lender holds legal title to the mortgaged property.

**ANSWER:** False. In a **lien theory** state (the majority rule), the borrower retains legal title and the lender holds only a security interest (lien) in the property. In a **title theory** state (the minority), the lender holds legal title until the loan is repaid.

---

**Q23**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is the equitable right of redemption, and what does it mean to "clog" it?

**ANSWER:** The equitable right of redemption is the borrower's right to pay off the debt and reclaim the property at any time *before* a foreclosure sale is completed. Courts of equity invented this doctrine to protect borrowers from harsh forfeiture. "Clogging the equity of redemption" means any attempt — in the original mortgage agreement or otherwise — to waive, limit, or eliminate this right. Courts treat such clauses as void; the borrower always retains the right to redeem pre-foreclosure.

---

**Q24**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is a Purchase Money Mortgage (PMM) and why does it get priority over other liens recorded before it?

**ANSWER:** A Purchase Money Mortgage is a mortgage given by a buyer to either the seller (seller-financed) or a third-party lender to finance the acquisition of the property itself. A PMM receives **super-priority**: it is treated as first in priority even over liens that attached to the property before the PMM was recorded. The rationale is that without the PMM, the buyer could not have purchased the property at all — the PMM actually funded the acquisition of the asset to which other liens would attach.

---

**Q25**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is a mechanic's lien, and why is its "relation-back" doctrine significant for lien priority?

**ANSWER:** A mechanic's lien is a statutory lien that protects contractors, subcontractors, and materialmen who improve real property. The relation-back doctrine makes the mechanic's lien priority date **relate back to the date work first began** on the project — not the date the lien was formally filed. This is significant because it can leapfrog liens recorded after work began but before filing, catching buyers and lenders who searched the records and found nothing at the time they recorded.

---

**Q26**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — When a senior mortgagee forecloses, the sale wipes out the senior mortgage itself and all junior interests.

**ANSWER:** False. Foreclosure by a **senior** lienholder wipes out all **junior** interests (junior mortgages, junior liens, junior easements) but does NOT wipe out the senior mortgage — the foreclosing senior lien is the one being paid off, and the buyer at the foreclosure sale takes free of all junior interests. Conversely, if a **junior** lienholder forecloses, the senior lien survives untouched.

---

**Q27**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What happens to the foreclosure sale proceeds? In what order are they distributed, and what is the result if the sale price is less than the outstanding debt?

**ANSWER:** Proceeds are distributed in lien priority order: (1) costs and expenses of the foreclosure sale, (2) senior liens (if not foreclosing), (3) the foreclosing lienholder, (4) junior lienholders in priority order, (5) any remaining surplus to the borrower. If the sale price is insufficient to pay the foreclosing lender in full, the shortfall is a **deficiency**. Under the modern approach, the deficiency = remaining debt minus the *fair market value* of the property (not the sale price), and the lender may sue the borrower personally on the note for that amount.

---

**Q28**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Who are "necessary parties" in a foreclosure action, and what happens if a necessary party is not joined?

**ANSWER:** Necessary parties are **junior lienholders** — anyone with an interest in the property that is junior to the foreclosing lien. They must be joined so they have notice and an opportunity to protect their interests (e.g., by bidding at the sale or paying off the senior lien). If a necessary party is not joined, their lien is **not extinguished** by the foreclosure — it survives and the buyer takes the property subject to it.

---

**Q29**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A property is sold at foreclosure by the first mortgagee (First Bank, owed $200,000). The sale price is $185,000. A second mortgage (owed $40,000) and a property tax lien ($5,000) also exist. How are the proceeds distributed, and what are the consequences for each party?

**ANSWER:** First, property tax liens have **super-priority by statute** and survive even senior mortgage foreclosures (or must be paid first). Distribution: (1) Property Tax Lien — $5,000. (2) First Bank (foreclosing lien) — receives $180,000 of its $200,000 debt. First Bank has a **deficiency of $20,000** and may sue the borrower on the note. (3) Second mortgage — $0 remaining; it is **wiped out** by the foreclosure. The second mortgagee may have a claim against the borrower personally if there was a personal guarantee, but loses its in rem security. The borrower receives no surplus.

---

**Q30**
> CATEGORY: Mortgages | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A lender holds a mortgage on a property and is owed $300,000. At the foreclosure sale, the lender submits a "credit bid" of $300,000 and wins the sale. What is a credit bid, and what are its practical effects for the lender and for junior lienholders?

**ANSWER:** A **credit bid** allows the foreclosing lender to bid up to the amount of its debt without paying cash — it simply offsets the bid against what is owed. Practical effects: (1) The lender takes title to the property without laying out cash. (2) Because the "sale price" equals the debt, there is no deficiency (or a reduced one). (3) Junior lienholders receive nothing from the sale proceeds (there is no surplus above the credit bid amount) and their liens are wiped out. (4) The lender now owns the property and must manage or sell it — a result lenders often prefer to avoid, which is why they may set the credit bid below full debt value.

## CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing

**Q31**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What are the minimum requirements for a valid deed?

**ANSWER:** A valid deed requires: (1) identification of the **grantor and grantee**, (2) a **legal description** of the property, (3) **operative words of grant** (words evidencing intent to transfer), and (4) **delivery** — the grantor's intentional act of transferring the deed with intent to pass title. Recording is not required for the deed to be valid between the parties. A habendum clause ("to have and to hold") is traditional but not universally required.

---

**Q32**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the difference between a general warranty deed, a special (limited) warranty deed, and a quitclaim deed?

**ANSWER:** A **general warranty deed** contains all six covenants and the grantor warrants title against defects from *any* source, including prior owners. A **special (limited) warranty deed** contains the present covenants plus quiet enjoyment, but the grantor warrants only against defects arising from *the grantor's own acts* — not those of prior owners. A **quitclaim deed** contains no covenants whatsoever — the grantor conveys only whatever interest they happen to hold (if any), with no guarantees.

---

**Q33**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — The Covenant of Seisin and the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment are both present covenants that are breached (if ever) at the moment of closing.

**ANSWER:** False. The Covenant of **Seisin** is a present covenant, breached at closing if the grantor doesn't actually own what they claim. The Covenant of **Quiet Enjoyment** is a *future* covenant — it is not breached at closing but only when a third party with paramount title actually asserts that title and interferes with the grantee's possession.

---

**Q34**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Name the three present covenants in a general warranty deed. What makes them "present" covenants?

**ANSWER:** The three present covenants are: (1) **Covenant of Seisin** — grantor owns what they claim to be conveying. (2) **Covenant of Right to Convey** — grantor has the legal authority to transfer. (3) **Covenant Against Encumbrances** — no undisclosed third-party interests (liens, easements, mortgages) burden the property. They are "present" because they are breached, if at all, **at the moment of closing** — the defect either exists or it doesn't. The statute of limitations runs from delivery.

---

**Q35**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Name the three future covenants in a general warranty deed and explain when they are breached.

**ANSWER:** (1) **Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment** — no one will disturb the grantee's possession. Breached when a third party with superior title actually asserts it and interferes with possession. (2) **Covenant of General Warranty** — no one holds superior rights; grantor will defend the grantee's title against all comers. Breached when superior title is asserted from any source. (3) **Covenant of Further Assurances** — grantor will take additional steps to perfect title if needed. Breached when the grantor refuses to execute a corrective instrument after a defect is discovered. Future covenants are breached only when paramount title is asserted — not merely by its existence.

---

**Q36**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** O sells Greenacre to A via a general warranty deed. O previously granted a utility easement to the power company, which was properly recorded. O did not mention this to A. Which present covenant, if any, is breached? What is A's remedy?

**ANSWER:** O has breached the **Covenant Against Encumbrances**. A recorded utility easement is a third-party interest burdening the property. Even though the easement was of record (and A may have had constructive notice), the covenant is breached if the encumbrance exists at closing. A can sue O for damages — typically the diminution in value attributable to the encumbrance, capped at the purchase price. Note: A's prior constructive record notice may affect whether A actually relied on the covenant, but at common law the covenant is still technically breached.

---

**Q37**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** A remote grantor (several links back in the chain of title) conveyed property with a general warranty deed. Can a current grantee sue the remote grantor for breach of a deed covenant?

**ANSWER:** It depends on the type of covenant. **Future covenants run with the land** — they travel down the chain of title and remote grantors can be sued for breach of quiet enjoyment, general warranty, and further assurances when paramount title is asserted against a current grantee. **Present covenants do not run with the land** (at common law) — they are breached, if at all, at the time of the original conveyance, and the original grantee's cause of action does not pass to subsequent grantees. **Damages are always capped at the original purchase price** paid by the conveying grantor.

---

**Q38**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What are the three types of legal descriptions used in American deeds? Give a one-sentence description of each.

**ANSWER:** (1) **Metes and Bounds** — describes the boundary of the parcel as a walk from a fixed point of beginning, using compass directions and distances. (2) **USGS (Government) Survey** — uses a grid system of townships (N-S) and ranges (E-W) divided into sections to locate parcels. (3) **Subdivision Plat** — identifies the lot by reference to a recorded plat map filed in the recorder's office (e.g., "Lot 12, Block 4, Maplewood Subdivision").

---

**Q39**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A buys Blackacre from O using a special warranty deed. Years later, a creditor of a prior owner (P, who owned Blackacre before O) records a judgment lien and seeks to foreclose. A sues O for breach of the general warranty covenant. Does A win? What about the covenant of quiet enjoyment?

**ANSWER:** A does **not** win on the **general warranty** covenant — O used a *special* warranty deed, which only covers defects arising from O's own acts. The judgment lien stems from a prior owner (P), not from O. O made no warranty against P's encumbrances. A also does not have a claim on **quiet enjoyment** under the special warranty deed for the same reason — the interference is caused by a prior owner's creditor, not O. A's only recourse is against P (if traceable) or potentially title insurance. This illustrates the critical risk of accepting a special or quitclaim deed.

---

**Q40**
> CATEGORY: Deeds & Conveyancing | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** O executes a deed conveying Blackacre to A and hands it to O's attorney with instructions: "Hold this and give it to A only if I die before her." O later changes their mind and demands the deed back. The attorney returns it. O dies. A sues O's estate for Blackacre. Who wins?

**ANSWER:** This turns on **delivery**. Delivery requires the grantor's intent to make a *present, unconditional* transfer of the deed. Here, O conditioned delivery on O's death — this resembles a testamentary transfer, which requires a will. However, if interpreted as a present transfer with enjoyment deferred until death (a valid deed condition), delivery to the attorney as escrow agent may constitute valid delivery. Most courts would find **no valid delivery** here because O retained control (evidenced by successfully demanding the deed back) — a grantor who can reclaim the deed has not made a present, irrevocable transfer. The escrow was illusory. O's estate likely wins.

## CATEGORY: Private Governance (Covenants & Servitudes)

**Q41**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** What is the fundamental difference between a contract and a real covenant? Why does this distinction matter?

**ANSWER:** A **contract** binds only the original parties — it is a personal obligation. A **real covenant** (promissory servitude) is a promise about land use that attaches to the land as a property interest, binding and benefiting future owners who were not party to the original agreement. This matters because land is frequently sold — the covenant "runs with the land" so that restrictions remain enforceable regardless of how many times the property changes hands.

---

**Q42**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — An equitable servitude requires proof of horizontal privity between the original covenanting parties for the burden to run.

**ANSWER:** False. Horizontal privity is required for the **burden** to run as a **real covenant at law**. Equitable servitudes require no horizontal privity — this is one of the key advantages of equitable servitude analysis. Instead, equity substitutes a **notice** requirement: the successor of the burdened land must have actual, record, or inquiry notice of the servitude.

---

**Q43**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What does it mean for a covenant to "touch and concern" the land? Give two examples of promises that satisfy this requirement and two that do not.

**ANSWER:** A promise "touches and concerns" the land if it relates to the **use, physical condition, or value** of the burdened or benefited parcel — not a purely personal obligation. **Satisfies:** a residential-only use restriction; a building height limit; a maintenance obligation for common areas; an exclusive-use clause in a commercial lease. **Does not satisfy:** a covenant to hire a specific contractor (personal to a particular entity — *Bremmeyer*); a covenant to buy water exclusively from a specific company (*Eagle Enterprises*). Courts strongly disfavor in-gross benefits; if the benefit is purely personal, the burden generally cannot run.

---

**Q44**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is horizontal privity, and which relationships satisfy it? Which common scenario does NOT?

**ANSWER:** Horizontal privity is the required relationship between the **original covenanting parties** at the moment the covenant is created — for the burden of a real covenant to run at law. It is satisfied by: (1) **Landlord-tenant** — the covenant is created within an existing lease. (2) **Grantor-grantee** — the covenant is created at the moment of deed delivery ("instantaneous privity"). The classic scenario that does NOT satisfy horizontal privity: **neighboring landowners** making a promise "over the back fence" — mere geographic proximity is not enough. This is why equitable servitude doctrine (which drops the horizontal privity requirement) is so commonly used.

**Q45**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 2 | [QUOTE]

**QUESTION:** "If the owner of the [Leicester Square] garden in question entered into a contract with the plaintiff that the garden should be used only as a garden, would it be right that the owner should be able to sell the garden to a purchaser who knew of the covenant, and then say that the covenant could not be enforced against him?" — What landmark case does this reasoning come from, and what legal rule did it establish?

**ANSWER:** This reasoning comes from **Tulk v. Moxhay** (England, 1848). The case established the doctrine of the **equitable servitude**: a restrictive covenant will be enforced in equity against a successor who takes land *with notice* of the restriction, even if the successor was not a party to the original covenant. The rule is that equity will not allow a person who purchases land with knowledge of a restrictive agreement to ignore it — conscience binds them to the restriction. This case is the foundation of equitable servitude doctrine.

**Q46**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Explain the constitutional history of racially restrictive covenants from *Corrigan v. Buckley* through *Shelley v. Kraemer*. What changed?

**ANSWER:** In ***Corrigan v. Buckley*** (1926), the Supreme Court held that a *private* racially restrictive covenant did not violate the 14th Amendment — private agreements are not state action. The Amendment only restricts government conduct. In ***Shelley v. Kraemer*** (1948), the Court held that while the *covenant itself* was private, **judicial enforcement** of a racially restrictive covenant by a state court constituted state action — and therefore violated the Equal Protection Clause. The result: courts could no longer enforce such covenants. *Barrows v. Jackson* (1953) extended this to bar awarding damages for breach of such a covenant. The Fair Housing Act (1968) made race-based covenants illegal and unenforceable by statute.

**Q47**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What is the Common Plan Doctrine, and what did *Sanborn v. McLean* (Mich. 1925) hold? What is the primary criticism of that holding?

**ANSWER:** The **Common Plan Doctrine** allows courts to imply that all lots in a residential development are burdened by reciprocal restrictions, even if a particular lot's deed contains no covenant, if: (1) lots are clustered, (2) most or all are burdened by similar covenants, and (3) there was a common grantor. In ***Sanborn v. McLean***, the Michigan court applied this doctrine to find an implied reciprocal negative easement binding a lot whose deed had no restriction — based on the uniform residential character of the neighborhood. The court held the buyer had **inquiry notice** because the neighborhood's residential character should have prompted investigation. The primary **criticism**: this undermines the recording system. The buyer in Sanborn was expected to search the title chains of *neighboring lots* — an obligation not required by normal title search practice — making title unmarketable and unpredictable.

**Q48**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** O owns a neighborhood development. O conveys Lot 1 to Adams with a deed covenant: "Adams and her heirs shall not display holiday decorations visible from the street." Adams conveys Lot 1 to David via a deed that contains no mention of this covenant, though it was recorded in O's original deed to Adams. David, who holds Lot 1 as a *life tenant*, displays a large holiday display. O's successor seeks to enforce. Analyze whether the burden runs at law and/or in equity.

**ANSWER:** **At law (real covenant):** (1) Writing ✓ (original deed). (2) Intent to run ✓ ("heirs"). (3) Touch & Concern — arguable; decorations restriction may touch and concern as a use restriction ✓. (4) Horizontal privity ✓ (O-Adams grantor-grantee). (5) Vertical privity (burden) — **FAILS**: David holds only a *life estate*; original promisor (Adams) held fee simple. Incomplete vertical privity bars the burden from running at law. **Fails as real covenant.** **In equity (equitable servitude):** (1) Writing ✓. (2) Intent ✓. (3) Touch & Concern ✓. (4) No horizontal privity required ✓. (5) Notice — the covenant was recorded in Adams's deed → **constructive/record notice** to David ✓. **Succeeds as equitable servitude.** Remedy is injunction only (no money damages in equity).

**Q49**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A Common Interest Community's Declaration (CC&Rs) includes a "no pets" rule. A unit owner who has owned her cat for years challenges the rule after the HOA seeks to enforce it against her specifically. She argues the rule is unreasonable as applied to her situation. How would a California court analyze this under *Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village*?

**ANSWER:** Under ***Nahrstedt*** (Cal. 1994), courts assess the reasonableness of HOA rules **in the abstract** — not as applied to a particular owner's circumstances. The rule is presumed valid if it is: (1) contained in the recorded CC&Rs, (2) not arbitrary (i.e., has a rational relationship to the protection of the common interest community), and (3) not illegal or against public policy. The fact that the rule may be harsh as applied to this particular owner (she had the cat before the rule was enforced, the cat is well-behaved, etc.) is **irrelevant** under the Nahrstedt standard. The court will not engage in case-by-case balancing. The "no pets" rule would be upheld because it rationally promotes quiet, cleanliness, and harmony in a multi-unit residential community.

**Q50**
> CATEGORY: Private Governance | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** A grocery store (Winn-Dixie) holds an exclusive-use covenant from a shopping center landlord that prohibits the landlord from leasing any other unit to a competing grocery store. Dollar General (a discount retailer that also sells groceries) leases a unit from the landlord. Winn-Dixie sues Dollar General to enforce the covenant. Analyze the key legal obstacles Dollar General would raise.

**ANSWER:** Dollar General would raise several arguments: (1) **Touch and Concern** — Does a non-compete covenant touch and concern the land? Under *Holmes's* view (*Norcross*), it does not — it only increases the benefitted land's value indirectly by excluding competition, which is a personal/economic benefit. Under the **modern majority** (*Whitinsville Plaza*), non-compete covenants in retail leases *do* touch and concern because they are standard features of commercial tenancies that directly affect permissible land use. (2) **Vertical Privity (Burden)** — Dollar General holds a *leasehold*, not a fee. In jurisdictions requiring **strict vertical privity** for the burden to run (e.g., Mississippi per *Winn-Dixie*), a lessee with a lesser estate than the original promisor (the fee-holding landlord) cannot be bound. (3) **Jurisdiction-Specific Rules** — Louisiana (civil law) may not recognize the covenant as a real obligation because a lease is not a title document. These obstacles explain why the *Winn-Dixie* litigation had different outcomes across three states.
