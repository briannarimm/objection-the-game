## CATEGORY 1: Statutory Foundations & Coverage

**Q1**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Under Title VII, what is the minimum number of employees required for an employer to be covered?

A) 5 employees
B) 10 employees
C) 15 employees
D) 20 employees

**ANSWER:** C) 15 employees. § 701(b) requires 15+ employees for each working day in 20+ calendar weeks in the current or preceding year. Compare: the ADEA requires 20+, the ADA requires 15+, and the Missouri MHRA requires just 6+.

---

**Q2**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Which of the following protected bases is NOT included in Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

A) National origin
B) Age
C) Religion
D) Color

**ANSWER:** B) Age. Title VII covers race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. Age discrimination is covered by a separate statute — the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA, 1967), which protects workers 40 and older.

**Q3**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Bostock v. Clayton County (2020)

**QUESTION:** After Bostock v. Clayton County (2020), which of the following correctly states the law?

A) Sexual orientation discrimination is covered only if the employer explicitly stated a discriminatory motive
B) Employers may discriminate based on sexual orientation if they have a sincere religious objection
C) Discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity constitutes sex discrimination under Title VII
D) LGBTQ+ employees are protected only in states that have enacted specific anti-discrimination laws

**ANSWER:** C) Discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity constitutes sex discrimination under Title VII. Justice Gorsuch's majority held: "It is impossible to discriminate against a person for being homosexual or transgender without discriminating against that individual based on sex."

**Q4**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 1 | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — Individual supervisors can typically be sued as named defendants under Title VII.

A) True — any person who discriminates can be sued individually
B) False — Title VII generally does not impose individual liability on supervisors

**ANSWER:** B) False. Individual supervisors are generally NOT covered defendants under Title VII — the employer entity is the proper defendant. Note: the old Missouri MHRA allowed supervisor personal liability, but 2024 MHRA amendments eliminated that.

**Q5**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: EEOC v. Sidley Austin (7th Cir. 2002)

**QUESTION:** Partners at a law firm argue they cannot be "employees" under the ADEA because they hold partner status under state law. Per EEOC v. Sidley Austin, how should courts resolve this?

A) Defer to state law — if they are partners under state law, they cannot be employees
B) Apply a functional analysis of actual control — whether the firm's executive committee can effectively fire, demote, or cut their pay without recourse
C) Presume all partners are employers, never employees, as a matter of federal common law
D) Count the number of partners; if they are a majority of firm members, they are employers

**ANSWER:** B) Functional analysis of actual control. Judge Posner held that state law partnership designation is NOT determinative. If an executive committee can effectively fire, demote, or cut pay without recourse, those "partners" may be "employees" under the ADEA.

**Q6**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Arbaugh v. Y&H Corp. (2006) | [TRUE/FALSE]

**QUESTION:** True or False — Under Arbaugh v. Y&H Corp. (2006), Title VII's 15-employee minimum is a jurisdictional requirement that a court must raise on its own if the employer is below the threshold.

A) True — courts must dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction at any time
B) False — the 15-employee minimum is not jurisdictional; it is a merits defense that can be waived

**ANSWER:** B) False. Arbaugh held the numerosity requirement is NOT jurisdictional — it is an element of the defendant's merits defense and can be waived if not timely raised. This is significant because jurisdictional defects can never be waived.

**Q7**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Which statute provides a private cause of action for race discrimination in contracts against private employers, with NO employee-size threshold and NO EEOC exhaustion requirement?

A) Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e
B) 42 U.S.C. § 1981
C) The ADEA, 29 U.S.C. § 621
D) The Missouri MHRA

**ANSWER:** B) 42 U.S.C. § 1981. This Reconstruction-era statute applies to private actors, requires no EEOC exhaustion, has no employee minimum, and provides uncapped damages. It is a powerful parallel vehicle for race discrimination claims.

**Q8**
> CATEGORY: Statutory Foundations & Coverage | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Robinson v. Shell Oil (1997)

**QUESTION:** Jane was fired and later received a deliberately negative reference from her former employer. She had already left the company when the reference was given. Which is correct?

A) Jane cannot sue because Title VII only protects current employees
B) Jane can sue — Robinson v. Shell Oil held that former employees are covered plaintiffs under § 704, and a retaliatory reference is actionable
C) Jane can sue under § 703 for the negative reference but not under § 704
D) Jane cannot sue because a negative reference is not a cognizable adverse action under any theory

**ANSWER:** B) Robinson v. Shell Oil (1997) held that former employees fall within § 704's protections. A deliberately negative reference by a prior employer to retaliate for a prior discrimination complaint is an actionable adverse action.

## CATEGORY 2: Proof Frameworks

**Q9**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine (1981)

**QUESTION:** In Step 2 of the McDonnell Douglas framework, what is the defendant's burden?

A) Prove by a preponderance that the decision was actually motivated by the legitimate reason
B) Produce evidence articulating a clear, reasonably specific legitimate, non-discriminatory reason — burden of production only
C) Present direct evidence disproving any discriminatory motive
D) Show that the plaintiff was the least qualified candidate considered

**ANSWER:** B) Burden of production only. Per Burdine, the defendant need not prove the articulated reason actually motivated the decision. The defendant just has to put a legitimate reason on the table — the burden of persuasion never shifts away from the plaintiff.

---

**Q10**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks (1993); Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing (2000)

**QUESTION:** At Step 3 of McDonnell Douglas (pretext), which statement is CORRECT?

A) If plaintiff disproves the defendant's articulated reason, plaintiff automatically wins
B) Plaintiff must show the articulated reason is pretextual — but disproving the reason alone may not be enough; plaintiff must still convince the factfinder that discrimination occurred
C) Plaintiff need only show the stated reason was factually inaccurate, regardless of the actual motive
D) Plaintiff must produce a prior victim of discrimination from the same employer

**ANSWER:** B) Per Hicks (1993), disproving the defendant's stated reason alone does not compel a verdict for plaintiff — they must still persuade the factfinder that discrimination occurred. However, Reeves (2000) held that a strong prima facie case combined with strong pretext evidence CAN be sufficient to support a jury verdict for plaintiff.

**Q11**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Griffith v. City of Des Moines (8th Cir. 2004)

**QUESTION:** The 8th Circuit's rule from Griffith v. City of Des Moines (2004) holds that at the summary judgment stage:

A) Both McDonnell Douglas and Price Waterhouse frameworks are available, and plaintiff chooses which to use
B) McDonnell Douglas is the only framework; if the evidence colorably suggests multiple motives, summary judgment is inappropriate and mixed-motive becomes a trial issue
C) The Price Waterhouse framework is preferred because it better reflects modern employment decisions
D) The court applies the motivating factor standard from § 703(m) directly, bypassing McDonnell Douglas

**ANSWER:** B) In the 8th Circuit, McDonnell Douglas is the only summary judgment framework. If the evidence suggests colorable mixed motives, summary judgment is denied and the mixed-motive question goes to trial. This is the 8th Circuit's distinct and exam-critical approach.

**Q12**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Desert Palace v. Costa (2003)

**QUESTION:** Desert Palace v. Costa (2003) changed the mixed-motive framework by holding that:

A) SCOTUS eliminated mixed-motive claims — plaintiff must always prove but-for causation
B) Direct evidence is required to trigger mixed-motive analysis under § 703(m)
C) Circumstantial evidence alone is sufficient to show a protected characteristic was "a motivating factor" — overruling the direct-evidence requirement from Justice O'Connor's Price Waterhouse concurrence
D) The "same decision" defense is unavailable whenever plaintiff uses only circumstantial evidence

**ANSWER:** C) Desert Palace held that circumstantial evidence suffices to trigger § 703(m) mixed-motive analysis. This effectively overruled the requirement in Justice O'Connor's Price Waterhouse concurrence that direct evidence be shown before the burden shifts to the defendant to prove the same-decision defense.

**Q13**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** Maria, a Latina woman, was passed over for a promotion in favor of a white man. Her supervisor had previously said women "lack the temperament for management." Her evidence is entirely circumstantial. At summary judgment, what is the proper framework and how does the comment factor in?

A) Price Waterhouse, because the supervisor's comment is direct evidence of discriminatory motive
B) McDonnell Douglas — circumstantial evidence triggers this framework; the supervisor's gender stereotype comment is admissible at Step 3 as pretext evidence
C) Disparate impact — the promotion decision had a statistical effect on women in the workplace
D) The claim fails at pleading because Maria cannot establish membership in a protected class as a Latina woman

**ANSWER:** B) McDonnell Douglas applies to circumstantial-evidence cases. The supervisor's "temperament" comment is a gender stereotype under Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins — it is relevant (and potentially powerful) pretext evidence at Step 3, especially if the supervisor was a decisionmaker. Stray remarks by non-decisionmakers carry less weight, but a decisionmaker's remark can be highly probative.

**Q14**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Ames v. Ohio Dept. of Youth Services (2025)

**QUESTION:** After Ames v. Ohio DYS (2025), what is the rule for majority-group plaintiffs (e.g., white or male employees) bringing Title VII disparate treatment claims?

A) They must show "background circumstances" suggesting their employer is the unusual employer who discriminates against the majority
B) They cannot bring Title VII reverse discrimination claims at all
C) They must make the same prima facie showing as any other plaintiff — no "background circumstances" are required
D) They must produce statistical evidence showing a pattern of discrimination against the majority

**ANSWER:** C) Ames eliminated the "background circumstances" requirement that some circuits had imposed on majority-group plaintiffs. The Supreme Court held that Title VII protects "any individual" and applies the same standard regardless of which group the plaintiff belongs to.

**Q15**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Gross v. FBL Financial (2009); Nassar (2013)

**QUESTION:** Which correctly states the causation standard for Title VII disparate treatment claims versus ADEA claims?

A) Both require but-for causation — same standard across all employment discrimination statutes
B) Title VII requires "motivating factor" (§ 703(m)); ADEA requires "but-for" causation (Gross v. FBL)
C) Title VII requires but-for causation; ADEA requires only a motivating factor
D) Both use the motivating factor standard after the 1991 Civil Rights Act

**ANSWER:** B) Title VII uses the "motivating factor" standard under § 703(m) (added by the 1991 CRA). The ADEA requires but-for causation, per Gross v. FBL (2009). Mixed-motive jury instructions are NOT available for ADEA claims. Retaliation under § 704 also requires but-for causation (Nassar, 2013).

**Q16**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** When a defendant successfully proves the "same decision" affirmative defense in a mixed-motive Title VII case, which remedies does § 706(g)(2)(B) permit the court to award?

A) Full remedies — back pay, compensatory and punitive damages, and reinstatement
B) Attorney's fees and costs only
C) Declaratory judgment, limited injunctive relief, and attorney's fees — but NOT back pay, compensatory/punitive damages, reinstatement, hiring, or promotion
D) No remedy at all — the plaintiff's claim is dismissed entirely

**ANSWER:** C) If the defendant proves the same-decision defense, § 706(g)(2)(B) is specific: the court MAY award declaratory judgment, limited injunctive relief, and attorney's fees. The court SHALL NOT award compensatory/punitive damages, back pay, reinstatement, hiring, or promotion. The plaintiff has proved a violation but receives limited relief.

**Q17**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** After the 1991 Civil Rights Act overruled Ward's Cove Packing, which party bears the burden of proving business necessity in a disparate impact case?

A) Plaintiff — must affirmatively disprove business necessity after establishing the statistical disparity
B) Defendant — must demonstrate the practice is job-related for the position in question and consistent with business necessity
C) The burden is shared equally between both parties
D) The EEOC — it must certify whether business necessity exists before the case can proceed

**ANSWER:** B) Post-Ward's Cove, Congress shifted the burden back to the defendant via § 703(k). The employer must prove the challenged practice is job-related and consistent with business necessity — this is an affirmative burden of proof, not merely production.

**Q18**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Under the EEOC's "4/5ths Rule," a presumptive showing of disparate impact exists when:

A) The pass rate for a protected group is less than 50% of the majority group's pass rate
B) The pass rate for the protected group is less than 80% of the highest group's pass rate
C) The overall workforce representation of a protected group is less than 80% of their proportion in the general population
D) Any test that results in fewer than 80% of all applicants passing, regardless of group breakdown

**ANSWER:** B) The EEOC 4/5ths (80%) Rule: disparate impact is presumed when the selection rate for any protected group is less than 4/5ths (80%) of the rate for the group with the highest selection rate. Example: white pass rate 65%, Black pass rate 40% → 40/65 = 61.5%, which is below 80% — presumptive disparate impact.

**Q19**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 3 | CASE NOTE: Ricci v. DeStefano (2009)

**QUESTION:** In Ricci v. DeStefano, New Haven discarded firefighter promotion test results because only white and Hispanic candidates scored high enough. The Supreme Court held:

A) The city was required to discard the results — once racial disparities appear, discarding is mandatory to avoid disparate impact liability
B) The city committed intentional race discrimination (disparate treatment) and could only have discarded results if it had a "strong basis in evidence" it would face disparate impact liability
C) The city's action was protected by the business necessity defense because avoiding disparate impact is always a legitimate goal
D) Black firefighters could not bring a disparate treatment claim because the city's policy applied equally to all races

**ANSWER:** B) Ricci is the collision point of disparate treatment and disparate impact. An employer may only make race-conscious employment decisions if it has a "strong basis in evidence" that it would face disparate impact liability. Discarding results solely because of the racial distribution of scores = intentional race discrimination (disparate treatment).

**Q20**
> CATEGORY: Proof Frameworks | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Staub v. Proctor Hospital (2011)

**QUESTION:** Under the "Cat's Paw" doctrine from Staub v. Proctor Hospital (2011), employer liability is established when:

A) The final decisionmaker was personally biased against the protected class
B) A biased lower-level supervisor performs an act intended to cause an adverse employment action, and that act is a proximate cause of the ultimate adverse action — even if the final decisionmaker was unbiased
C) Any manager in the chain of command held discriminatory beliefs at any time during the employee's tenure
D) The plaintiff can prove the biased supervisor's views "contaminated" the final decision through unconscious influence

**ANSWER:** B) Cat's Paw doctrine: if a biased supervisor commits an act intended to cause adverse action and that act is a proximate cause of the ultimate adverse decision, the employer is liable — even if the final decisionmaker was unbiased. However, if the final decisionmaker conducts a genuinely independent investigation that provides sufficient justification without relying on the biased source, causation may be broken.

## CATEGORY 3: Harassment & Retaliation

**Q21**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: Burlington Industries v. Ellerth (1998); Faragher v. Boca Raton (1998)

**QUESTION:** Under the Ellerth/Faragher framework, when may an employer raise the two-part affirmative defense to supervisor harassment?

A) Always, regardless of whether there was a tangible employment action
B) Only when the employer can prove the harassment never occurred
C) Only when there was NO tangible employment action by the supervisor
D) Only when the harassment was perpetrated by a co-worker, not a supervisor

**ANSWER:** C) The Ellerth/Faragher affirmative defense is available ONLY when no tangible employment action was taken. When a supervisor takes a tangible employment action (firing, demotion, pay cut, etc.), employer liability is automatic and vicarious — no affirmative defense is available.

---

**Q22**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** The Ellerth/Faragher two-prong affirmative defense requires the employer to show:

A) (1) The employer had no actual knowledge of the harassment, and (2) the harasser was disciplined within 30 days
B) (1) The employer exercised reasonable care to prevent and promptly correct harassing behavior, and (2) plaintiff unreasonably failed to take advantage of preventive or corrective opportunities
C) (1) The harassment was not "because of sex," and (2) the conduct was welcomed by plaintiff
D) (1) The harasser believed the conduct was welcome, and (2) the employer ended harassment when notified

**ANSWER:** B) Both prongs are required: (1) reasonable care to prevent and correct — demonstrated through an effective anti-harassment policy with a bypass provision, investigation procedure, and non-retaliation guarantee; and (2) plaintiff's unreasonable failure to use those corrective procedures. If plaintiff reports through proper channels, prong 2 defeats the defense.

**Q23**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway v. White (2006)

**QUESTION:** After Burlington Northern v. White (2006), a retaliatory action is "materially adverse" for § 704 purposes if:

A) The action causes direct economic harm (e.g., pay cut or termination)
B) The action falls within the list of covered employment-related practices in § 703(a)
C) The action would dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a Title VII complaint
D) The action is identical in type and severity to the original discriminatory act

**ANSWER:** C) Burlington Northern broadened retaliation's adverse action standard beyond employment-related actions. Materially adverse = would dissuade a reasonable worker from complaining. Context matters — a schedule change is trivial for most employees, but could be materially adverse for a single parent who relies on a specific schedule.

**Q24**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Thompson v. North American Stainless (2011)

**QUESTION:** Thompson v. North American Stainless (2011) held that:

A) Only the employee who filed the original charge has standing to bring a retaliation claim
B) Firing a co-worker to retaliate against a charging party is not actionable retaliation under Title VII
C) Firing the fiancé of a Title VII complainant can constitute unlawful retaliation, and the fiancé has standing to sue as a person within the "zone of interests" the statute protects
D) Third-party retaliation requires direct evidence of a retaliatory motive to be actionable

**ANSWER:** C) Thompson extended retaliation protection to third parties closely connected to a complainant. The Court held that firing an employee's fiancé to punish the employee for filing a charge falls within § 704(a)'s zone of interests. The statute aims to deter retaliation broadly, not just protect the original complainant.

**Q25**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** Aaron, a white employee, tells HR he believes his supervisor is racially harassing a Black colleague. Two weeks later, Aaron is passed over for a promotion he was clearly qualified for. Does Aaron have a retaliation claim?

A) No — retaliation protection only covers the actual victim of the underlying discrimination
B) Yes — opposing discriminatory practices is protected activity under § 704's Opposition Clause even if Aaron is not the victim; temporal proximity supports but-for causation
C) Yes — but only if Aaron also experienced racial discrimination himself
D) No — opposition to discrimination must be in writing to be protected under § 704

**ANSWER:** B) § 704's Opposition Clause protects any employee who opposes an unlawful practice — they need not be the victim. Aaron's complaint to HR is protected opposition activity. Retaliation requires but-for causation (Nassar), and two weeks between the complaint and adverse action is strong circumstantial evidence. Note: Aaron's claim is under § 704, not § 703 — the Burlington Northern "materially adverse" standard for adverse actions applies.

**Q26**
> CATEGORY: Harassment & Retaliation | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services (1998)

**QUESTION:** Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services (1998) held that:

A) Same-sex harassment is never actionable under Title VII because Congress intended the statute to address male-on-female harassment
B) Same-sex harassment is actionable under Title VII if the harassing conduct was directed at the victim "because of sex"
C) Same-sex harassment is only actionable if the harasser is proven to be homosexual or was motivated by sexual desire
D) Same-sex harassment requires a pattern of conduct and cannot be established by a single incident

**ANSWER:** B) Oncale held that same-sex harassment is cognizable under Title VII. The central question is always whether the conduct was directed at the victim "because of sex" — sexual desire is one way to show this, but not the only way (e.g., general hostility toward one sex, comparative evidence that the harasser treated members of the other sex differently).

## CATEGORY 4: Protected Characteristics

**Q27**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: PDA (1978)

**QUESTION:** The Pregnancy Discrimination Act (PDA) of 1978 added § 701(k) to Title VII and requires employers to:

A) Provide pregnant employees with up to 12 weeks of paid maternity leave
B) Treat pregnant employees the same as employees "similar in their ability or inability to work"
C) Provide reasonable accommodation to all pregnant workers regardless of cost or business impact
D) Assign a temporary replacement for any pregnant employee who takes leave

**ANSWER:** B) The PDA requires that pregnancy-related conditions be treated the same as other conditions that similarly limit ability to work. Accommodation is not directly required by the PDA — that duty came later with the PWFA (2022), which mirrors ADA reasonable accommodation language.

**Q28**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Young v. UPS (2015)

**QUESTION:** In Young v. UPS (2015), a pregnant UPS driver requested a light-duty accommodation (consistent with her physician's lifting restrictions) that UPS provided to workers with on-the-job injuries but denied to her. How does the Court analyze this?

A) Strict scrutiny — any denial of accommodation to a pregnant worker is presumptively unlawful
B) The ADA framework applies directly to pregnancy accommodation requests post-Young
C) A modified McDonnell Douglas analysis — if the employer accommodates comparable workers (similar inability to work), it must provide a significant justification for refusing a pregnant employee's identical request
D) Full employer discretion — accommodation for pregnancy is entirely optional under the PDA

**ANSWER:** C) Young v. UPS: Plaintiff shows the employer accommodates others similar in inability to work (comparators) but not pregnant employees → shifts burden to employer to offer a significant justification → plaintiff may rebut by showing the justification is pretextual. This is a modified McDonnell Douglas framework tailored to pregnancy accommodation.

**Q29**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: TWA v. Hardison (1977); Groff v. DeJoy (2023)

**QUESTION:** Groff v. DeJoy (2023) clarified the religious accommodation "undue hardship" standard by holding that:

A) Undue hardship means any cost whatsoever — even a single dollar spent on accommodation is too much
B) The Hardison "more than de minimis" standard remains the correct test, and Groff simply reaffirmed it
C) Undue hardship requires something genuinely "hard to bear" — assessed in the context of the employer's overall operations; the casual "de minimis" language from Hardison was not its controlling holding
D) Undue hardship can only be established by showing financial cost, not operational disruption

**ANSWER:** C) Groff clarified that undue hardship is more demanding than Hardison's loose "de minimis" language suggested. Courts must evaluate whether accommodation would genuinely impose substantial burdens considering the employer's size, nature, and overall operating costs. The easy "de minimis" escape hatch is narrower than previously understood.

**Q30**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: EEOC v. Abercrombie & Fitch (2015)

**QUESTION:** EEOC v. Abercrombie & Fitch (2015) addressed a job applicant who wore a hijab. The employer did not hire her, fearing her hijab would violate its "Look Policy." The Supreme Court held:

A) An employer must always ask applicants about religious accommodation needs before making a hiring decision
B) An applicant must formally request a religious accommodation before the employer's refusal to hire becomes actionable
C) An employer violates Title VII if a discriminatory motive (avoiding accommodation) was a factor in the decision — even without a formal accommodation request by the applicant
D) Religious garb cannot form the basis of a Title VII claim because dress codes are facially neutral

**ANSWER:** C) Abercrombie held that Title VII prohibits acting on an assumption that accommodation will be needed. The employer's motive is what matters — if the employer refuses to hire because it suspects it will need to make a religious accommodation, that is discriminatory intent regardless of whether the applicant formally requested one.

**Q31**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 1 | CASE NOTE: General Dynamics v. Cline (2004)

**QUESTION:** Which of the following is true about the ADEA's protection?

A) The ADEA protects all employees from any form of age-based discrimination
B) The ADEA protects employees 40 years of age and older; it does NOT protect younger workers who are discriminated against in favor of older workers
C) The ADEA originally covered workers between 40 and 70, and that age cap was never removed
D) The ADEA protects employees over 65 who face forced mandatory retirement policies

**ANSWER:** B) The ADEA covers employees 40 and older. Per General Dynamics v. Cline (2004), "age" under the ADEA means old age — a 40-year-old who claims discrimination in favor of a 55-year-old cannot use the ADEA because the statute does not protect against discrimination favoring older workers over younger ones.

**Q32**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 3

**QUESTION:** Under the ADAAA (2008), if an employee takes medication that fully controls their condition, how should courts assess whether the employee is "disabled" under the ADA?

A) Evaluate the disability as it exists while the employee takes their medication (mitigated state)
B) Evaluate the disability in its unmitigated state — mitigating measures like medication are disregarded, except for ordinary eyeglasses or contact lenses
C) Average the employee's level of impairment with and without medication
D) The ADAAA eliminated the "substantially limits" requirement entirely, so medication is irrelevant

**ANSWER:** B) The ADAAA overruled Sutton v. United Air Lines (1999), which required evaluation in the corrected/mitigated state. Now, disability is assessed without regard to mitigating measures — medications, hearing aids, prosthetics, etc. — except ordinary eyeglasses or contacts. This dramatically expanded the class of people who qualify as "disabled."

**Q33**
> CATEGORY: Protected Characteristics | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Espinoza v. Farah Manufacturing (1973)

**QUESTION:** In Espinoza v. Farah Manufacturing (1973), the Supreme Court addressed whether citizenship discrimination equals national origin discrimination. The holding was:

A) Citizenship discrimination is the same as national origin discrimination under Title VII
B) An employer who refuses to hire non-citizens discriminates based on national origin
C) Discrimination based solely on citizenship status — not national origin — is not prohibited by Title VII, though IRCA separately addresses it
D) Employers may never inquire about citizenship status before making a hiring decision

**ANSWER:** C) Espinoza drew a clear line: citizenship ≠ national origin under Title VII. A policy requiring U.S. citizenship for all employees is not per se national origin discrimination. However, the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA, 1986) independently prohibits discrimination against work-authorized non-citizens and prohibits hiring unauthorized workers.

## CATEGORY 5: Defenses & Remedies

**Q34**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 1

**QUESTION:** Which of the following characteristics can NEVER serve as a BFOQ under § 703(e) of Title VII?

A) Sex
B) Religion
C) Race
D) National origin

**ANSWER:** C) Race is NEVER a BFOQ — it is not listed in § 703(e), which permits BFOQ only for religion, sex, or national origin. This is an absolute rule with no exceptions under any circumstance.

---

**Q35**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: Wilson v. Southwest Airlines (1981)

**QUESTION:** In Wilson v. Southwest Airlines (1981), the court rejected Southwest's BFOQ defense for hiring only female flight attendants. The key reasoning was:

A) The FAA prohibits any gender-based hiring decisions for crew members
B) The BFOQ test requires the protected characteristic to be necessary to the "essence of the business" — safe transport of passengers — not to Southwest's sex-appeal marketing image
C) Customer preference for female flight attendants can never be considered in a BFOQ analysis as a matter of law
D) Southwest's hiring policy was facially neutral and therefore only analyzed under disparate impact, not BFOQ

**ANSWER:** B) Wilson established the "essence of the business" test: sex must relate to the core mission of the enterprise, not to a peripheral marketing strategy. The court held: "Sex does not become a BFOQ merely because an employer chooses to exploit female sexuality as a marketing tool."

**Q36**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 3 | CASE NOTE: US Airways v. Barnett (2002)

**QUESTION:** In US Airways v. Barnett (2002), a disabled employee requested reassignment to a less physically demanding position that was ordinarily awarded by seniority. Under the ADA, the Supreme Court held:

A) The ADA always requires the employer to give the position to the disabled employee regardless of seniority
B) Federal law preempts seniority systems, so seniority is irrelevant when accommodation is at stake
C) Making an exception to a bona fide seniority system is presumptively unreasonable as an accommodation, but the employee can overcome this by showing special circumstances (e.g., the employer regularly makes exceptions to the system)
D) The plaintiff automatically loses if the employer can produce a written seniority policy

**ANSWER:** C) Barnett: A requested accommodation that conflicts with a bona fide seniority system is presumptively not "reasonable" — because seniority systems generate legitimate reliance interests among other workers. But the presumption is rebuttable: if the employee shows the employer regularly makes exceptions to seniority for other reasons, the accommodation might still be reasonable in the particular case.

**Q37**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 2 | CASE NOTE: McKennon v. Nashville Banner (1995)

**QUESTION:** Under McKennon v. Nashville Banner (1995), what happens when an employer discovers — after firing an employee for discriminatory reasons — that the employee had committed misconduct that would have justified termination?

A) After-acquired evidence completely bars the plaintiff's discrimination claim from the outset
B) After-acquired evidence has no effect — the discriminatory motive has already occurred
C) After-acquired evidence does not bar the claim but limits available remedies: reinstatement and front pay are barred, and back pay is limited to the period from discharge to the date the evidence was discovered
D) After-acquired evidence shifts the burden to plaintiff to disprove the alleged misconduct

**ANSWER:** C) McKennon: After-acquired evidence does not eliminate the discrimination claim — discrimination still occurred. But remedies are limited: reinstatement and front pay are inappropriate, and back pay runs only from the date of discharge to the date the employer discovered the misconduct. The employer must show it would have terminated the employee had it known.

**Q38**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** What remedies are available to a plaintiff who prevails on a disparate impact claim under Title VII?

A) Compensatory damages, punitive damages, back pay, and injunctive relief — same as a disparate treatment claim
B) Equitable relief only — injunctions, back pay, declaratory judgments; NO compensatory or punitive damages
C) Compensatory damages only — punitive damages are never available in Title VII cases
D) The same remedies as a disparate treatment claim, because the 1991 CRA made no distinction

**ANSWER:** B) Disparate impact claims yield only equitable relief. Compensatory and punitive damages are available only in intentional discrimination (disparate treatment) cases. This is a critical exam distinction — DI remedy = equitable only; DT under Title VII post-1991 = compensatory + punitive damages (subject to caps).

**Q39**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 3 | [HYPO]

**QUESTION:** Employer fires an employee, citing "poor performance." In discovery, plaintiff finds: (1) no formal performance reviews exist, (2) a non-protected comparator with worse objective metrics was retained, and (3) a supervisor used racially coded language in internal emails. Under McDonnell Douglas, what is the strongest analysis?

A) Step 1 fails because plaintiff has no "direct evidence" of discrimination — McDonnell Douglas requires direct evidence
B) Plaintiff establishes a prima facie case; employer meets Step 2 by articulating poor performance; at Step 3, qualification disparity + coded language + lack of documentation collectively support pretext — under Reeves, this evidence can permit a jury verdict for plaintiff
C) The claim fails at Step 3 because "coded language" without an explicit slur is legally insufficient to show pretext
D) The lack of formal performance reviews automatically defeats the employer's articulated reason — plaintiff wins at Step 2

**ANSWER:** B) This is a classic pretext scenario. At Step 3, pretext can be shown by the combination of: (1) a comparator with objectively worse performance who was retained (qualification disparity); (2) coded racial language suggesting discriminatory motive; and (3) lack of documentation undermining credibility of the stated reason. Under Reeves, this combination can survive summary judgment and support a jury verdict for plaintiff — none of these elements alone is required to be dispositive.

**Q40**
> CATEGORY: Defenses & Remedies | DIFFICULTY: 2

**QUESTION:** Under the Missouri Human Rights Act (MHRA), the causation standard for discrimination claims is:

A) "A motivating factor" — identical to federal Title VII's § 703(m) standard
B) "But-for" causation — the same as the ADEA under Gross v. FBL
C) "The motivating factor" — meaning the protected characteristic must have been a determinative influence on the adverse action; stricter than federal Title VII
D) A negligence standard — the employer must have known about the discrimination and failed to prevent it

**ANSWER:** C) The Missouri MHRA uses a "the motivating factor" standard under MAI 38.06 — the protected characteristic must be a determinative influence on the decision. This is stricter than federal Title VII's "a motivating factor" standard. Combined with the MHRA's lower 6-employee threshold and post-2024 amendments, Missouri law is a distinct track from federal law.
